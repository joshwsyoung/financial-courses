**Scenario.** Tom is an equity analyst at Northgate Investors, a fictional Leeds asset manager. At a family barbecue, his brother-in-law mentions that his employer, a listed company called Halden plc, will issue a profit warning next week. Northgate's funds hold Halden shares. Tom also owns some in his own ISA. Back at work, a fund manager asks Tom whether Halden is still a hold.

**Step 1: identify the facts.** A profit warning would very likely move the share price, so the information is material. It has not been announced, so it is non-public. Tom did not seek it out, but he now has it.

**Step 2: find the Standards involved.**
Standard II(A) bars acting on, or passing on, material non-public information.
Standard III(A) asks Tom to be loyal to clients. That may tempt him to protect the funds by selling.
Standard VI(B) means he must never put his own trades ahead of clients'.

**Step 3: list the options.**
(a) Sell his own ISA holding quietly.
(b) Tell the fund manager to sell, to protect clients.
(c) Say nothing, and keep rating Halden a hold.
(d) Do not trade or pass it on, and tell compliance straight away.

**Step 4: test each option.**
(a) Trading on inside information for himself. A clear breach of Standard II(A), and a likely offence under UK insider dealing law. **Fails.**
(b) Selling for clients is still using inside information. Loyalty to clients does not override market integrity. **Fails.**
(c) No trade, but Tom is now giving a view he knows may be misleading, and compliance cannot manage the risk. **Weak.**
(d) Protects the market and lets compliance act. It may add Halden to a restricted list and decide what to say to the fund manager. **Passes.**

**Step 5: decide, act and reflect.** Tom chooses (d). He tells the fund manager he cannot comment on Halden for now. He does not explain why.

**Takeaway:** the duty to clients works *inside* the rules on market integrity, never around them. You cannot use inside information, even to help a client.
