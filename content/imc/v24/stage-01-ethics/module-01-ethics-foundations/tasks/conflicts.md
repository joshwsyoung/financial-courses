A **conflict of interest** exists when you, your firm or another client could gain from a decision in a way that might pull you away from doing the best thing for the client in front of you.

Common examples in investment firms:

- **Remuneration:** a bonus tied to selling one product rather than another.
- **Gifts and hospitality:** a broker offers tickets to a big match.
- **Personal dealing:** you buy a share just before recommending it to clients.
- **Client against client:** two clients both want a limited allocation of a popular new issue.
- **In-house products:** the firm earns more if clients use its own funds.

Having a conflict is not, on its own, a breach. Conflicts are a normal part of business. The problem is **failing to manage** them.

Firms usually manage conflicts in this order of preference:

1. **Avoid** the conflict where possible, for example by refusing large gifts.
2. **Control** it, for example with information barriers between teams, or rules that clients trade before staff.
3. **Disclose** it clearly to the client, so they can decide with full knowledge.

Disclosure is the **last resort**, not a cure-all. If a conflict is too serious to be managed fairly, telling the client about it does not make it acceptable.

**Precise definition:** a conflict of interest is a situation in which a duty owed to a client could be compromised by a competing interest of the firm, its staff or another client.
